What's actually driving the growth

Tuition vs. services as share of due process payments
In FY2012, tuition was 74% of due process payments. By FY2023, services (speech, OT, PT, SETSS, paraprofessionals delivered by private providers) had overtaken tuition as the largest category.

Caveat: The Department of Education cannot disaggregate Carter (reimbursement-based) from Connors (prospective / direct payment) spending. In March 2025 testimony before the City Council, DOE General Counsel confirmed the data is not structured to separate them. This means it is not possible, from public data, to measure whether Connors has succeeded at its original purpose — leveling access for families who cannot front tuition.

Where the money goes: specialized private schools commonly cited in Carter placements

No public dataset lists which private schools receive Carter or Connors payments, or how much each receives annually. Obtaining that breakdown would require a FOIL request to DOE for payee-level tuition disbursements. What is published is each school's own annual tuition — shown below for schools widely cited in legal guides, journalism, and DOE Council testimony as serving students placed under IDEA due process. Inclusion on this list is not an allegation; these are schools known to enroll Carter-placed students, and most say so publicly.

What this table is not. These are the schools' own published annual tuition figures for 2025–26 (or most recent available), not per-student Carter receipts. Actual settlements paid by DOE frequently exceed the published tuition because they can include related services (speech, occupational therapy, physical therapy), 1:1 paraprofessionals, transportation, and legal fees. The FY2024 average Carter settlement of $101,757 is higher than all but a handful of published tuitions on this list, which is consistent with settlements bundling services on top of tuition.

Why the curve bent in 2014

In September 2014, the de Blasio administration simplified the settlement process: a 15-day target to settle, a policy of not re-litigating previously settled cases, and reduced paperwork requirements. Settlement volume jumped 61% in the first full year. The reform was a response to a documented dysfunction — families who had won FAPE-denial findings were being dragged back into hearings every September — but it created a one-way ratchet: placements approved once tended to continue indefinitely, with annual tuition escalation, under IDEA's "stay-put" pendency protections.

What this tracker can and can't show

Publicly available (tracked here)

  • Total annual spending, from IBO fiscal briefs, the City Comptroller's reports, the State Comptroller (OSC), the Mayor's Management Report, and DOE budget testimony at City Council.
  • Category split (tuition vs. services vs. legal vs. transportation) from the Comptroller's Course Correction report (FY2012–FY2023).
  • Total case counts and timeliness from the Mayor's Management Report.

Not publicly available

  • Carter vs. Connors split — DOE itself cannot produce this.
  • School-by-school spending distribution — which private schools absorb what share.
  • Demographic breakdown of filing families (FOIL-able but not published).
  • Outcomes data on private placements — academic, behavioral, or post-secondary.
  • District-level availability of District 75 programs and ICT seats, year over year.
