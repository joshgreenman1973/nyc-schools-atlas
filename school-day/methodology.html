Methodology and data: New York City school time, 2026–27

Every figure on the page, where it came from and how it was computed. Revised Oct. 7, 2026; the corrections are listed at the end.

What we measured

Scheduled hours in school per year: the length of the standard school day, bell to bell, including lunch and recess, multiplied by the number of days students are scheduled to attend. This is how Kraft and Novicoff built the national average the page compares against (6.9 hours a day × 178.6 days).

It is not New York State’s measure. The state requires 180 days of session and a minimum of 900 instructional hours a year for kindergarten through grade 6 and 990 for grades 7–12, and its definition of instructional hours “shall not include ... lunch or recess” (8 NYCRR 175.5). Nothing on this page says whether New York City meets or misses those hourly minimums.

Notes on the week grid

  • Grade bands follow the calendar’s own definitions: elementary schools serve kindergarten through grade 8 (including 3-K and Pre-K); middle schools serve grades 6–8; high schools serve grades 9–12. District 75 schools follow the middle school conference dates. K–12 schools follow the high school conference dates and the elementary clerical day, and the grid does not show them separately.
  • Regents exam days (Jan. 26–29 and June 15–25) are counted as interruptions only for high school. The calendar says Regents days “are school days” but that students in grades 7–12 “may have modified schedules”; Chalkbeat reports that many high school students do not attend on those days.
  • Election Day (Nov. 3) is a remote instruction day for all students. It counts toward the 177 days but is shown as a break in the in-person week.
  • David Bloomfield of Brooklyn College and the CUNY Graduate Center told Chalkbeat in February 2026 that, out of 45 school weeks, about 20 have complete five-day weeks. Chalkbeat reported that in paraphrase, about the 2025–26 calendar. We did not reproduce his count and do not use it on the page.

The “Build your own school year” calculator

The calculator uses the same formula as the headline: annual_hours = school_day_minutes × student_attendance_days ÷ 60. At the New York City defaults (380 minutes × 177 days) it returns 1,121 hours. The “K–12 cumulative hours” readout multiplies the per-year gap against the 1,231-hour U.S. average by 13 years. It is a simple projection, not a forecast. The U.S. button sets 414 minutes and 179 days, the nearest whole-number settings to 6.9 hours and 178.6 days, which gives 1,235 hours.

The Success Academy button sets 468 minutes, the network’s average elementary day across a week (four days from 7:50 a.m. to 4:20 p.m. and a Wednesday dismissal at 12:50 p.m.), and 182 days, the count for returning third and fourth graders. That gives 1,420 hours; our day-by-day count for those grades is 1,400 because their first three days are half days.

Caveats

The K–12 total is illustrative. It assumes the 2026–27 schedule holds for 13 years and that a student attends every scheduled day. The national comparison figure is from 2017–18, the year Kraft and Novicoff used.
The Kraft and Novicoff review is national, not specific to New York City. Across 74 studies with causal designs, they find “a compelling body of evidence that increasing total school time leads to gains in academic achievement, on average.” They do not estimate effects for New York City.
States count school time differently. New York State excludes lunch and recess from its 900- and 990-hour minimums. Texas requires districts to operate at least 75,600 minutes a year, “including time allocated for instruction, intermissions, and recesses for students” (Texas Education Code § 25.081). California sets annual instructional minutes by grade, from 36,000 for kindergarten to 64,800 for grades 9–12 (Education Code § 46207). Florida counts 720 net hours for kindergarten through grade 3 and 900 for grades 4–12 (Florida Statutes § 1011.61). The scheduled hours used here are a common yardstick across systems; they are not what each state’s regulators measure.
Charter schools set their own schedules. New York City Public Schools says “Charter schools follow their own calendars.” Charter schedules on the chart describe what students in those schools are scheduled for; they do not show what the city’s district schools could adopt.
Spending. Chalkbeat reports that New York City public schools “spend well above average per student.” The Citizens Budget Commission’s January 2025 report “Highest Costs, Middling Marks” covers New York State as a whole: it found per-student spending 91 percent above the national average in 2021–22 and estimated $36,293 per student in 2024–25.

Corrections

Oct. 7, 2026: A fact-check against the sources above changed the page. Earlier versions:

  • Presented Chalkbeat’s 2025–26 figure of 1,102 hours as the 2026–27 figure. The 2026–27 calendar works out to 1,121 scheduled hours, a 110-hour gap rather than 129.
  • Said the gap adds up to “roughly a year and a half” over K–12. The earlier figure divided by an unsourced 1,150-hour year; on the study’s own convention the old numbers gave 1.4 years, and the 2026–27 numbers give 1.2.
  • Listed the national study’s authors as “Kraft, Goldstein & Lyon.” The authors are Matthew A. Kraft and Sarah Novicoff, and the 1,231-hour figure comes from 2017–18 federal survey data, not from their review of 74 studies.
  • Called the national school day seven hours and the city’s day about 40 minutes shorter. The study’s figure is 6.9 hours, about 35 minutes longer than the city’s.
  • Ran the two halves of a Kraft quote together in reverse order, with an added subject. The quote now matches Chalkbeat’s text.
  • Showed a week grid that applied every grade band’s early dismissals, staff days and Regents days to all students, left out the March early dismissals and showed June 21 as a regular day. The grid now follows the calendar for each grade band.
  • Counted 9 Regents days (there are 13), 3 conference early dismissals (each student has 2), 5 religious or cultural closures (4 religious holiday closures) and an 11-week summer that depends on a 2027–28 calendar not yet published.
  • Explained the calculator’s 1,121 hours as reflecting “three days of weather/snow waivers in 2025–26.” There was one snow day that year. The calculator also had a conference-day slider that did not change the result; it was removed.
  • Charted peer figures that did not match the systems’ own schedules: Success Academy at 1,570 hours (no Wednesday half days; 185–190 days), KIPP NYC at 1,380 (one school’s day, no early dismissals), Houston at 1,260 (the Texas legal minimum) with 187 days (181), Chicago at 1,055 (a 7-hour elementary day gives 1,232), Miami-Dade at 1,170 (no Wednesday early release; last day June 3, not June 4), and Los Angeles with Houston’s start and end dates. Uncommon Schools’ 1,250 could not be verified because the network does not publish bell times.
