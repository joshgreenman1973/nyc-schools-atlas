What New York City kids are expected to know, K through 12

Curriculum, the lessons and materials a school uses, can differ from school to school. The state’s learning standards are the same everywhere. Here is what New York State says students should be able to do by the end of each grade band in the four core subjects, paraphrased from its English, math and science standards and its social studies framework.

Why this exists Every school teaches reading, math, science and social studies, but the curriculum (the textbooks, lessons and sequence) can differ. In New York City that gap has narrowed. Under NYC Reads, every district elementary school now uses one of three approved reading curricula (EL Education, Wit & Wisdom or HMH Into Reading), and as of fall 2026 the program also covers middle schools in 18 of the 32 community school districts and a first group of high schools. Under NYC Solves, district high school math classes use a single curriculum, Illustrative Mathematics; middle schools in 25 of the 32 districts, and elementary schools in four, use a curriculum from an approved list. These mandates cover district schools, not charter or private schools. The state standards sit underneath all of it: they define what a student is supposed to be able to do, whichever curriculum got them there, and the state tests are built on them. Sources & provenance

Each cell’s summary is paraphrased from the published New York State learning standards. The examples were written for this page to illustrate the standard; they are not quoted from the standards, which do not prescribe specific books, essay formats, page lengths or numbers of sources. The standards documents are the authoritative source — this page is a guide to them, not a replacement.

  • English language arts: NYS Next Generation English Language Arts Learning Standards (adopted September 2017; instruction phased in from September 2022 for prekindergarten through grade 8 to September 2025 for grade 11; state grade 3–8 tests aligned to them since spring 2023 and the English Regents exam since June 2026).
  • Mathematics: NYS Next Generation Mathematics Learning Standards (adopted September 2017; instruction phased in from September 2019 for prekindergarten through grade 2 to September 2025 for Algebra II; grade 3–8 tests aligned since spring 2023, and the Algebra I, Geometry and Algebra II Regents exams since June 2024, June 2025 and June 2026).
  • Science: NYS P-12 Science Learning Standards (adopted December 2016; based on the Next Generation Science Standards; new grade 5 and grade 8 tests since spring 2024; new Regents exams in Earth and Space Sciences and Life Science: Biology since June 2025 and in Chemistry and Physics since June 2026).
  • Social studies: NYS K-12 Social Studies Framework (introduction dated 2014; the K–8 and 9–12 sections were last revised in 2017).
  • New York City curricula: NYC Public Schools pages for NYC Reads and NYC Solves, which list the curricula and the districts in each phase.
  • Diploma requirements: State Education Department graduation requirements and its Sept. 14, 2026, announcement on moving to one diploma.

Diploma rules are changing. Today a Regents or local diploma requires 22 credits and passing one Regents exam or approved alternative in each of English, math, science and social studies, plus one more “pathway” assessment. In September 2026 the Board of Regents authorized the State Education Department to move to a single state diploma and drop the requirement to pass Regents exams, beginning with students graduating in 2028; the department plans to propose the regulations by February 2027. Regents exams will continue as statewide tests. Separately, a state rule adopted in March 2026 requires personal finance instruction in grades 5 through 12 starting in 2026–27 and in kindergarten through grade 4 starting in 2027–28; climate education follows a year later at each level.

Honest limitation: standards are not curriculum. A school that exceeds standards looks very different from one that barely meets them, even if both are technically “on grade level.” This page tells you the floor, not the ceiling.
