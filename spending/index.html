New York City public schools: spending, fiscal years 2016 to 2025

Per-pupil K-12 spending in New York City: where the money goes

Among the 100 largest school districts in the United States, New York City spent the most per pupil in fiscal year (FY) 2024, the latest year the U.S. Census Bureau has published. This page shows how that figure is built, how much of its growth comes from shrinking enrollment and where the money goes. The figures come from the Census Bureau, the New York City Independent Budget Office (IBO), the City Council's Finance Division, the Department of Education (DOE), the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES). Each chart names its source. City fiscal years run July to June, so FY2024 is the 2023-24 school year.

1. The climb since FY2016: district schools only, measured the same way each year

This series uses one definition throughout: the Census Bureau's current spending per pupil for the New York City School District, from its Annual Survey of School System Finances. Current spending covers day-to-day costs, including salaries, health insurance and pension contributions. Census leaves out capital outlay, debt service and the department's payments to charter schools and private schools. Enrollment is the fall count of students in DOE schools reported to the federal Common Core of Data (845,509 in fall 2023), which includes some pre-K pupils and no charter students. Use the buttons to switch between per-pupil and total spending, and between dollars as spent and dollars adjusted for inflation.

Why FY2022 spikes and FY2023 dips. In the Census figures, per-pupil spending rose 20 percent in FY2022, to $35,914 from $29,931, as current spending rose 13 percent and enrollment fell 5.9 percent. It fell to $33,387 in FY2023 and was $35,796 in FY2024. IBO's figures show the same peak and dip. They also show federal aid to the department nearly doubling in FY2022, to $6.1 billion from $3.3 billion (in 2025 dollars), as pandemic relief arrived, then falling in FY2023 and again in FY2025. Neither source assigns the swing to one cause.

Charter schools are not in this series. About 151,000 New York City students attended charter schools in 2025-26, according to DOE's demographic snapshot, which lists 284 charter schools. Charter schools are separate districts in federal data, and the Census Bureau's survey excludes charter schools whose charters are held by nongovernmental entities, which describes New York's.

How this differs from IBO's $38,814. IBO's per-pupil figure for FY2025 divides all DOE-related spending (operations, charter payments, payments for private and contract-school placements, pensions, debt service and other health care costs) by every student DOE pays for, including charter students, 3-K and pre-K children at community-based centers and contract-school students: 1,071,581 in FY2025. Census leaves out debt service, capital and payments to charter and private schools, and counts only district-school students. Both include pension contributions and health insurance. The two are close in size, but they are not the same measure. Over the long run, IBO's per-pupil figure, adjusted for inflation, has roughly doubled, from $18,782 in FY1990 to $38,814 in FY2025 (2025 dollars). IBO notes that the city refined its enrollment count for FY2025, which makes comparisons with FY2024 difficult.

2. Denominator effect: how much of the rise is just fewer pupils?

Per-pupil spending is a ratio: total dollars on top, enrollment on the bottom. From FY2016 to FY2024, district spending rose and district enrollment fell, so per-pupil spending grew faster than spending itself. In dollars as spent, a third to two-fifths of the per-pupil rise comes from the smaller denominator, depending on how it is measured. After inflation, nearly all of it does.

Indexed to FY2016 = 100. District enrollment fell 13.9 percent, from 981,667 in FY2016 to 845,509 in FY2024, a loss of 136,158 students. About 71 percent of that drop came between the fall 2019 and fall 2021 counts (FY2020 to FY2022). Total district current spending rose 27.9 percent in dollars as spent and 2.1 percent after inflation. Per-pupil spending rose 48.5 percent in dollars as spent and 18.5 percent after inflation. Sources: Census Bureau, Annual Survey of School System Finances, Table 18, FY2016-FY2024; total spending is the Census per-pupil figure times Census enrollment. Inflation adjustment: consumer price index for all urban consumers (CPI-U), New York-Newark-Jersey City, July-June averages, Bureau of Labor Statistics.
Why costs do not fall with enrollment. Salaries and employee benefits made up 70 percent of the Census per-pupil figure in FY2024 ($25,161 of $35,796). Staffing has not shrunk with enrollment: IBO counts 120,720 full-time DOE pedagogical positions in FY2025, up from 115,799 in FY2016, while the enrollment it counts fell from 1,169,730 to 1,071,581 (IBO says the FY2025 count was refined). IBO's ratio of students to pedagogical staff fell from 12.1 in FY1990 to 8.9 in FY2025. That ratio is not a class-size measure: its student count includes charter students, its staff count includes only DOE employees and the pedagogical category includes paraprofessionals as well as teachers. A 2022 state law requiring smaller classes in New York City, phased in starting in 2023-24, adds to staffing needs.
Decomposition, FY2016 to FY2024. In dollars as spent, per-pupil spending rose $11,687, or 48.5 percent. Had enrollment stayed at its FY2016 level of 981,667, FY2024's $30.27 billion in district current spending would have come to $30,831 per pupil instead of $35,796. By that measure, $4,965, or 42 percent of the rise, reflects fewer students. Measured the other way, spreading FY2016's spending over FY2024's enrollment, the share is 33 percent. After inflation the picture changes: district current spending was 2.1 percent higher in FY2024 than in FY2016, so about 87 to 89 percent of the $5,600 real increase in per-pupil spending comes from the smaller denominator.
A broader measure moves differently. IBO's full-cost series includes charter payments, pensions and debt service, and its enrollment count includes charter students, whose numbers grew. In 2025 dollars, IBO's total rose 12.9 percent from FY2016 to FY2024 while its enrollment count fell 5.4 percent, so fewer students account for roughly 30 percent of its 19.4 percent real per-pupil increase.

3. The $41.6 billion, broken down (IBO, FY2025)

IBO's Oct. 6, 2026, update covers FY2025 actual spending. Its total includes DOE's operating spending plus three DOE-related costs that other city agencies manage. Figures are dollars as spent.

What each slice is. School-related programs ($19.5B): IBO's grouping of general and special education instruction in district schools, charter school payments, pre-K, early childhood programs and categorical (grant-funded) programs. Fringe benefits budgeted centrally ($4.2B): health insurance, Social Security, welfare-fund and other benefits for DOE employees in budget line U/A 461; about $0.65 billion more in benefits sits inside other lines. All other operating ($10.8B): District 75 and other centrally run special education, special education pre-K contracts, contract schools and tuition-reimbursement (Carter) cases, transportation, school food, facilities, safety, energy and leases, school support organizations and central administration; net of $0.12 billion in services sold to other city agencies. Pensions ($3.4B), debt service ($3.4B) and other health care costs ($0.2B): DOE-related costs that other city agencies manage, outside DOE's operating budget. Source: IBO, Education Spending Since 1990, data file updated Oct. 6, 2026.
Inside school-related programs ($19.5B), FY2025. IBO's six components, by budget line (unit of appropriation, or U/A): general education district schools (U/A 401/402), charter schools (406), special education district schools (403/404), categorical programs (481/482), pre-K (407/408) and early childhood (409/410). IBO's Oct. 2026 release rounds the total to $20 billion. The special education line covers instruction in district schools, such as integrated co-teaching, special classes and resource room; it does not include District 75 or the other special education lines in Section 6.
About per-pupil math on single lines. An earlier version of this page divided the general education line by an enrollment estimate. IBO cautions against that kind of calculation: "users should use some caution as not all categories of spending apply to all students reported in that total enrollment." This page no longer shows it.

4. Schools vs. central administration vs. everything else

DOE's operating budget is divided into units of appropriation (U/As), and IBO publishes actual spending for each. Grouped into six buckets, central administration (U/A 453/454) was 1.3 percent of DOE operating spending in FY2025. In IBO's figures that share stayed between 1.2 and 1.8 percent every year from FY2011 to FY2025.

FY2025 actual spending by U/A, dollars as spent (total $34.7 billion, before netting out $0.12 billion in sales to other city agencies). School-related programs ($19.5B) = U/A 401-410 and 481/482, IBO's grouping. Fringe benefits ($4.2B) = U/A 461. Central special education and private placements ($5.1B) = U/A 421-424 (District 75 and other citywide special education plus centrally managed related services such as occupational and physical therapy), 470 (special education pre-K contracts), 472 (contract schools, Carter cases, foster care) and 474 (non-public schools and the Fashion Institute of Technology). Operations ($5.0B) = U/A 435-444: facilities, pupil transportation, school food, safety, energy and leases. School support organizations ($0.39B) = U/A 415/416: superintendents' offices, borough support and family engagement. Central administration ($0.45B) = U/A 453/454: DOE headquarters offices. Shares in the tooltips are of the $34.67 billion total of all U/As. Source: IBO, Education Spending Since 1990 data file (Oct. 6, 2026), sheet E. An earlier version of this chart used the City Council Finance Division's report on the FY2019 preliminary budget (March 2018), which put U/A 453/454 at $345 million of a $25.6 billion budget, or 1.35 percent. That was a budget figure; IBO's actual-spending figure for FY2019 is 1.41 percent.
Why fringe benefits are their own bar. Most health insurance, Social Security and welfare-fund costs for DOE employees are budgeted on one central line, U/A 461, rather than charged to schools or offices. The budget does not say how much of it covers school staff versus central staff, so it is shown separately. Pensions are not in DOE's budget at all.
If you're looking for a big central-bureaucracy story, the operating budget isn't it. Headquarters administration was about 1.3 percent of operating spending in FY2025; adding school support organizations brings administration to about 2.4 percent. The large categories are school-related programs (56 percent), centrally budgeted fringe benefits (12 percent), centrally run special education and private placements (15 percent) and operations such as transportation, food and facilities (14 percent). Pensions, debt service and other health care costs, another $7.0 billion, sit outside the operating budget. Two cautions: some administrative work is paid from other lines, and starting with the FY2026 budget the city created new U/As 433/434 for DOE's technology division while U/A 453/454 shrank by a similar amount, so budgets from FY2026 on are not directly comparable with these figures.

5. Where each dollar goes inside a school

The most detailed public breakdown of New York City per-pupil spending by function was DOE's School Based Expenditure Report. DOE's last edition covers FY2018 (the 2017-18 school year), so this chart shows FY2018 in dollars as spent that year.

Source: NYC DOE, School Based Expenditure Report, FY2018 System Wide Report #1 (run Aug. 6, 2019). Per-student amounts for public schools, using DOE's enrollment of 1,021,229 (879,907 general education and 141,322 full-time special education students). "Direct services to schools" (the first five bars plus a $3 "other" line) totals $22,170 per student. "System-wide obligations" is mostly debt service ($2,132 per student) and retiree health and welfare ($713); pension contributions for current staff are counted within the other categories. All public-school functions total $26,266 per student. Pass-throughs of $4.75 billion (charter schools $2.39 billion; non-public schools $2.29 billion, of which $2.04 billion was special education; the Fashion Institute of Technology $0.06 billion) are not included in the per-student amounts.
Why this matters. No DOE report since FY2018 splits per-pupil spending into classroom instruction, school leadership, support services and system overhead this way. DOE still files New York State school funding transparency forms, and the state report card lists school-level per-pupil spending by funding source, but neither uses these functional categories. IBO's budget-line data (Sections 3 and 4) are the closest current substitute and do not map one-to-one to classroom versus administration.

The first five lines total $7.5 billion in FY2025, before fringe benefits, pensions and special education busing, which are budgeted elsewhere. U/A 472 also pays some costs that are not special education, such as schooling for foster children placed outside the city. The FY2025 due-process figure is lower than in earlier years partly because DOE and the city budget office changed how these costs are booked: costs are now charged to the year a case is decided or settled rather than charged back to the year it began. In the FY2027 budget, due-process cases moved to their own line, U/A 476.

What is not broken out publicly

  • District 75 per-pupil spending. District 75 serves students with significant disabilities in all five boroughs; DOE counted 30,694 students in District 75 in 2025-26, up from 25,937 in 2021-22. Its schools are funded outside the Fair Student Funding formula; DOE says they are funded "based on a class model." DOE does not publish an annual District 75 per-pupil figure.
  • Carter and Connors cases, separately. In Carter cases the city reimburses families who paid private tuition; in Connors cases it pays the school directly for families who cannot pay up front. DOE's general counsel told the City Council's education committee on Jan. 30, 2025, "We don't track the distinction between Carters and Connors" (unofficial transcript, citymeetings.nyc). DOE does separate tuition from services in its case-level reports to the Council.
  • Which private schools are paid. DOE's case-level report to the Council (December 2025) lists payment amounts and student ZIP codes but not the schools paid.
  • A total. No public document adds up special education instruction, District 75, related services, private placements, due-process costs, transportation and benefits into one figure.

7. Why is New York City so much higher? Decomposing the $18,000 gap

In FY2024, New York City spent $35,796 per pupil and the national average was $17,619 (Census current spending, dollars as spent), a gap of $18,177. A common explanation is that the city has more students with disabilities. It does, but under a commonly cited cost assumption that difference explains roughly 5 to 12 percent of the gap. Most of the gap is in instruction, spread across salaries, benefits and other costs.

Part A: New York City has a higher share of students with disabilities

Sources and years differ by bar. City districts: U.S. Department of Education Civil Rights Data Collection, 2021-22, students served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) as a share of K-12 enrollment, via the Urban Institute Education Data Portal; New York City's figure covers DOE schools, not charters. States and nation: NCES, Digest of Education Statistics Table 204.70, students ages 3-21 served under IDEA as a share of public school enrollment, 2022-23. DOE's own count for 2023-24 is 22.4 percent of district-school students with an Individualized Education Program (IEP), including 3-K and pre-K in the denominator; for 2025-26 it is 24.2 percent.

Part B: Where the gap is, by type of spending

Per pupil, FY2024, dollars as spent. Census Bureau, 2024 Annual Survey of School System Finances: Table 18 for New York City and Table 8 for the United States. "Other" is current spending that is not salaries or employee benefits, such as purchased services, contracts and supplies. Of the $18,177 gap, $6,533 is salaries and wages, $4,700 is employee benefits and $6,944 is other spending. By function, instruction accounts for $15,746 of the gap (87 percent) and support services for $2,469. Census notes that New York City reports guidance counselors under instruction, which slightly overstates its instruction spending and understates support services.
How much could the special education share explain? A rough calculation: suppose a student with disabilities costs 1.9 times as much to educate as other students, the ratio estimated in the federally funded Special Education Expenditure Project for 1999-2000, the most recent national estimate. Raising the U.S. share of students with disabilities (15.2 percent) to New York City's (22.4 percent, or 23.5 percent in the federal civil rights data) would add about $1,000 to $1,200 per pupil at national cost levels, or about $1,900 to $2,200 at New York City's. That is roughly 5 to 12 percent of the $18,177 gap. This is an illustration resting on an old national ratio, not a measurement; no government source divides the gap into causes.
Other documented differences, not apportioned. New York State had 11.7 students per teacher in fall 2022, compared with 15.4 nationally (NCES, Digest Table 208.40). The average New York City school building is 75 years old (IBO, March 2025). Census figures are not adjusted for regional differences in wages or prices.

8. How New York City compares to other large districts

Among the 100 largest U.S. school systems, New York City had the highest current spending per pupil in FY2024, the most recent year the Census Bureau has published. It ranked first in every year from FY2016 to FY2024 except FY2021, when Boston, then among the 100 largest, ranked first.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, 2024 Annual Survey of School System Finances (released May 7, 2026), Table 18, current spending per pupil, and Table 8 for the national average. All figures FY2024, dollars as spent, not adjusted for regional cost differences. Boston is shown for reference; with 45,742 students it is not among the 100 largest systems. Its figure is calculated from the Census district file the same way Census calculates Table 18 (current spending minus payments to charter and private schools, divided by fall enrollment), a method that reproduces Table 18 exactly for every other district shown. Current spending excludes capital outlay and debt service, so it is a narrower measure than IBO's full-cost figure.
